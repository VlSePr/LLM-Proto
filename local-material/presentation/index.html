How big is 1.5 billion? If each knob were a grain of rice, you'd have about 30 tonnes of rice. On disk, the knobs for chatting fit in about 3 gigabytes: less than a movie. For scale, GPT-3 had 175 billion knobs, and today's open models like Llama 3 come in 8 and 70 billion. Ours is small, but it's the same kind of machine.

What does “d_model 2048” mean? Every word, on every floor, is described by 2048 numbers. Think of 2048 little dials describing the word in context: is it a noun, is it happy, is it about food, is it the end of a sentence. Nobody labels the dials; the model invents what they mean.

This is the whole model. Read it from the bottom up, like a building.

At the bottom, the words come in: “The cat sat on the”. Each word is turned into a list of 2048 numbers. That's the embedding, like giving every word its own ID card with 2048 facts on it.

Then there are 32 floors, the blocks. Running up through the middle is a glowing pipe: the residual stream. Think of it as a notebook that gets carried up the building. On every floor, two workers read the notebook and write extra notes into it. They never erase anything, they only add. Notice the pipe gets thicker near the top: the notes keep piling up.

Each floor has two rooms. The purple room is attention: the words look at each other. The pink room is the feed-forward: each word thinks on its own. At the very top, the notebook is turned into a score for every word it knows, and the highest bars win. Here: “mat”.

  • Slab sizes follow parameter counts: SwiGLU is wider than attention (~77% of a block)
  • Stream thickness follows the measured activation std per layer (≈3 → ≈24, read from the activation chart)
  • The early / middle / late descriptions are what probing studies usually find, not something we measured

Two small changes, big stability win

RMSNorm & SwiGLU

RMSNorm

LayerNorm: (x − μ) / σ · g + b
RMSNorm: x / √(mean(x²) + ε) · g
  • No mean subtraction and no bias, so it's cheaper and just as stable
  • Pre-norm: applied before each sub-layer, so the residual stream stays raw
  • Computed in fp32 even under bf16 autocast

SwiGLU feed-forward

FFN(x) = Wdown( SiLU(Wgatex) ⊙ Wupx )
  • A learned gate decides, per dimension, how much signal passes
  • 3 matrices, so hidden = 8/3·d instead of 4·d keeps the parameters equal
  • 2048 → 5632 → 2048: 34.6M parameters per layer
⏱ core 1 min · extended 2 min · skip if short on timeby the end of this slide: ~10 min core / ~18 min extended

Two small parts that make a big difference. RMSNorm is a volume knob. Some numbers in the notebook get very loud, some very quiet. Before a room reads the notebook, RMSNorm turns everything to a normal speaking volume, so nobody shouts and nobody whispers.

SwiGLU is the thinking room, with gates. The idea gets spread out into 5632 little channels, and each channel has a door that decides how far to open, a little or a lot, depending on the input. Then everything is squeezed back down. Those doors are what make it smarter than a plain layer of the same size.

  • RMSNorm: no mean, no bias, computed in fp32 (Zhang & Sennrich 2019)
  • SwiGLU: W_down( SiLU(W_gate x) ⊙ W_up x ), 2048 → 5632 → 2048, 34.6M params per layer (Shazeer 2020)
  • Together they're the difference between a GPT-2 block and a LLaMA block
If you have time (+1 min)

Why does the volume knob matter so much? Imagine a message passed through 32 people. If each one speaks a little louder, by the end it's a scream; a little quieter, and it's silence. Deep networks have exactly that problem. The volume knob before each room keeps every room hearing a normal voice, no matter how loud the notebook has become.

And why a smooth gate? Older networks used ReLU: a door that's either open or slammed shut. SiLU is a door that can be a bit open, and learning works much better when small changes have small effects.

Interactive · position as rotation

RoPE: rotary position embeddings

Each pair of q/k dimensions is rotated by an angle proportional to the token's position. After rotation, q·k depends only on the distance m − n, not on where the pair sits in the sequence.

query pos m key pos n dim pair i
Watch the dot product stay the same
⏱ core 1.5 min · extended 3 minby the end of this slide: ~11 min core / ~21 min extended

Here's a problem: the model sees all the words at once, like a bag of Scrabble tiles. How does it know which word came first? “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” use the same tiles.

RoPE's trick uses clock hands. Each word gets an arrow, and the arrow is turned by an amount that depends on its seat number: seat 1 turns a little, seat 5 turns more. When two words compare arrows, the angle between them tells them how far apart they sit.

The magic: if you move both words along by the same amount [click Shift], both arrows spin, but the angle between them stays the same. The model only cares about the distance, not the exact seat. That's why it doesn't need to learn anything here: zero parameters.

  • θᵢ = 10000^(−2i/64): pair 0 turns 1 rad/token, the slowest ~1e-4
  • Fast pairs = nearby order, slow pairs = long range
  • q·k depends only on m − n

Do: Drag m and n and watch the relative angle change. Then click “Shift both +12”: arrows spin, the cosine stays fixed.

If you have time (+1.5 min)

How did older models do this? GPT-2 kept a table of 1,024 learned positions, one per seat. Seat 1,025 simply didn't exist, so it couldn't read longer text. RoPE has no table: seat 5,000 is just a bit more rotation. That's one reason modern models can stretch to long documents.

Why many clocks at different speeds? One fast clock can tell neighbours apart but spins around too often to measure long distances. One slow clock is the opposite. So we use 32 clocks from fast to slow, like the second, minute and hour hands on a watch together.

⏱ core 1.5 min · extended 2.5 minby the end of this slide: ~13 min core / ~24 min extended

Attention is like a classroom. Every word raises its hand with a question (the query), every word wears a name tag (the key) saying what it's about, and has a message (the value). A word listens most to the classmates whose name tags match its question.

Now, when the model writes a long answer, it has to remember every classmate's name tag and message for every word so far. That's the KV cache, and it gets heavy, like a backpack. Grouped-query attention says: 4 students can share one textbook. 32 questions, but only 8 name-tag sets. Backpack four times lighter, almost no loss in quality.

  • MHA at 4k context, batch 8: 8 GB of cache, more than the weights (3 GB bf16)
  • GQA (8 KV): 2 GB. MQA (1 KV): 256 MB, but quality drops
  • Formula: 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × tokens × batch × 2 bytes
  • LLaMA-2-70B, Mistral and others use GQA

Do: Toggle MHA / GQA / MQA, then drag context and batch.

If you have time (+1 min)

Why keep a cache at all? When the model writes word 101, it needs to look back at words 1 to 100. Without a cache it would recompute all of them for every new word, like re-reading the whole book before writing each sentence. The cache stores each word's name tag and message once, so each new word only does its own work.

That's also why long chats get slower and hungrier: the backpack grows with every word. Tricks like GQA are how models afford long conversations.

uint16 is enough: vocab 32,000 < 65,536, so each token takes 2 bytes and gets memory-mapped straight into batches.
Cheap to check: the manifest + shard sizes are verified before any training step. A stale cache can't sneak in.
Colab-proof: VMs die. Tokenizing billions of tokens again costs hours; downloading shards takes minutes.
⏱ core 1 min · extended 2 minby the end of this slide: ~14 min core / ~26 min extended

Training is like cooking every day. You don't want to chop the vegetables again every morning. So we chop everything once, put it in labelled boxes, and freeze it. That's the pipeline: download the text, cut it into tokens once, store the numbers in big files called shards, and keep them on Google Drive.

The label on the box is a fingerprint. If anything about the recipe changes, even one file, the label changes and we chop again. Otherwise we just grab the frozen box. The training code never chops anything.

  • Sources: HF streaming, text dirs, JSONL; pretrained on FineWeb-Edu + Project Gutenberg
  • uint16: 32,000 < 65,536, so each token is 2 bytes and memory-mapped straight into batches
  • 100M-token shards + val.bin (every 200th doc) + manifest.json
  • Drive cache at tokenized/<fingerprint>/: a new Colab VM downloads instead of re-tokenizing

If asked: Datasets from other sources? fetch_prebuilt_dataset pulls a folder as-is and only checks the tokenizer hash.

If you have time (+1 min)

Why be so strict about the fingerprint? Because the most painful bug in this whole project would be silent: training on text chopped with a different tokenizer. The model would learn garbage, the loss would still go down a bit, and you'd lose days before noticing. The fingerprint makes that impossible.

And one in every 200 documents is set aside and never trained on. That's the exam paper we use to check the model honestly. More on that with the loss curves.

⏱ core 1.5 min · extended 3 minby the end of this slide: ~15 min core / ~29 min extended

Models don't read letters or words. They read tokens: pieces of words, like Lego bricks. Common words are one brick, rare words are built from several bricks. Our model knows 32,000 different bricks.

Look at the colours: red means the model was surprised. It's surprised by “Ten”, “Pass” and “Ratified”, but once it has seen “United”, the word “States” is nearly free. The model is spending its effort on the new information, just like you do when you read.

  • “The United States Bill of Rights” = 6 tokens, not 32 characters
  • Ġ = leading space: ĠUnited and United are different tokens
  • Byte fallback: emoji, Cyrillic, code all encode, no <unk>
  • ChatML markers <|im_start|> <|im_end|> reserved from day one

If asked: Why can't LLMs count letters? Because they never see letters, only bricks. A word like “strawberry” is a few bricks, not ten letters.

If you have time (+1.5 min)

How are the bricks invented? It's a simple game called BPE. Start with single letters. Find the pair that appears most often next to each other, say “t” and “h”, and glue it into a new brick “th”. Repeat: “th” + “e” becomes “the”. Keep gluing until you have 32,000 bricks. Common words end up as one brick; rare words stay in pieces.

That explains a lot of LLM quirks. Numbers get split into odd chunks, so arithmetic is hard. A word with a space in front and without one are different bricks. And spelling questions are hard, because the model never sees letters.

Mini exercise: ask the room how they'd split “unbelievably”. There's no single right answer, and that's the point: it depends on what was common in the training text.

A famous example from older word-embedding models: take the point for “king”, subtract “man”, add “woman”, and you land near “queen”. Directions in this space can mean things like gender, plural or tense. The model was never told these rules, they fall out of predicting the next word.

One more detail: the same table is used twice. At the bottom it turns bricks into points; at the top the model compares its final notebook to every point to decide which brick comes next. So the map is both how words enter and how they leave.

src/train.py

The training loop

01

Optimizer

AdamW, β = (0.9, 0.95). Two param groups: no weight decay on norms, biases and 1-D params. Gradient clip 1.0.

02

Schedule

Linear warm-up, then cosine decay to min_lr (10% of peak). Warm-up keeps early, poorly-directed updates small.

03

Precision

precision: auto → bf16 on Ampere+ (and on our Blackwell), fp16 + GradScaler on older GPUs, fp32 on CPU.

04

Speed

torch.compile on CUDA (auto-off on Colab), flash SDPA, gradient accumulation. Gradient checkpointing must go in before compile.

for step in range(start_step, max_steps):          # start_step = ckpt["step"] + 1
    lr = get_lr(step, warmup, max_steps, peak_lr, min_lr)
    for micro in range(grad_accum):                 # 12 × 10 sequences per optimizer step
        loss = model(x, targets=y)["loss"] / grad_accum   # + aux_loss_coeff · aux_loss for MoE
        scaler.scale(loss).backward()
    clip_grad_norm_(params, 1.0); scaler.step(opt); opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
⏱ core 1 min · extended 2.5 minby the end of this slide: ~21 min core / ~39 min extended

This is the whole lesson plan, in four ideas. AdamW is the teacher that decides how much to nudge each knob. The schedule is how big the nudges are: gentle at the start (warm-up, like stretching before a run), big in the middle, and smaller and smaller at the end so it settles down. Precision: we store numbers with fewer digits when that's accurate enough, which is faster. And speed tricks so the GPU never waits.

The code at the bottom is the whole loop. Guess, measure how wrong, nudge the knobs, repeat.

  • Linear warm-up then cosine to min_lr (10% of peak)
  • bf16 on Ampere+ / Blackwell; fp16 + GradScaler on older GPUs
  • torch.compile on CUDA (auto-off on Colab); gradient checkpointing must go in before compile

If you have time (+1.5 min)

Back to the foggy mountain. If your steps are too big, you jump over the valley and end up on the other side, even higher. Too small, and you'll still be walking next year. That's why the step size is planned: gentle warm-up, big strides in the middle, tiny careful steps at the end.

Two safety rails. Gradient clipping is a speed limit: if the slope suddenly looks like a cliff, we cap the step so one strange batch can't throw the model off the mountain. Weight decay is gentle forgetting: knobs that aren't useful slowly drift back to zero, which keeps the model from memorising noise.

Colab will kill your machine. So we save the game, like in a video game. But a normal save isn't enough: we want to continue exactly where we were, same next page, same dice rolls. Otherwise two runs slowly drift apart.

So every save stores the knobs, the teacher's memory, the random-number dice, and the exact page we were reading. When we resume, we fast-forward to that page without re-reading the book.

  • A test trains N steps straight vs N/2 + interrupt + resume and asserts identical weights

The real story: early in the project, a resumed run behaved slightly differently from an uninterrupted one. Tiny differences, but in training tiny differences grow. Tracking it down led to saving the dice (random states) and the exact page, and to a test that fails if the two ever disagree. Boring engineering, but it's what lets a $120 run survive a dozen restarts.

Different heads learn different jobs. Researchers have found heads that look at the previous word, heads that match brackets and quotes, and so-called induction heads that spot “A B … A” and predict “B” again: the model's copy-and-continue trick (Olsson et al., 2022). Nobody programs these; they emerge from the guessing game.

That copy trick is also why a model repeats names and phrases from your prompt so reliably, and why it sometimes loops.

The point of this slide: these aren't mysterious AI ghosts. Every failure has a cause we've already seen today.

Glued words? The repetition penalty pushed away all the normal words that start with a space, so only word-pieces were left, and they stick together. CAPITAL LETTERS? Once it starts shouting, shouting is the most likely next move. Invented turns? It learned the play-script format so well it plays both roles. Wrong topic? The long shared chat history outvoted a short, misspelled question. Dangerous medical nonsense? It learned the tone of medical text, not the facts.

And sometimes, for 1.5 billion knobs, it's genuinely lovely.

  • Tokenizer + sampling, mode lock-in, chat format, context bleed, fluency ≠ truth
  • Glued words is our best hypothesis; it shows up with repetition penalty ≥ 1.5 on long generations

If asked: “So it hallucinates?” It always generates the most plausible continuation. It has no separate notion of true. Fluency is not truth.

If you have time (+1 min)

How would you fix each one? Glued words: a lower or windowed repetition penalty, which is what we do now. CAPITAL LETTERS: cleaner data and a bit more reading. Invented turns: proper stop markers in the fine-tuning data, and cutting the reply at the first fake “user”. Wrong topic: shorter history or clearer prompts. And for facts: give the model the facts at question time, which is what retrieval systems (RAG) do, instead of hoping it memorised them.

One more idea, just a peek. Instead of one thinking room per floor, you can have several specialists and a receptionist who sends each word to the best one or two. The building knows more, but each word still only visits one or two rooms, so it's not slower. That's how Mixtral and DeepSeek grow.

I did it after training: copied the thinking room into specialists on the top 8 floors and taught them video-game lore.

  • Warm start: experts copy the dense FFN, so the output is unchanged at graft time
  • Freeze the rest; router + experts train; grow one expert per round
  • Switch-style load-balance loss (coefficient 0.01) stops the router collapsing
If you have time (+1 min)

Why is this attractive? Knowledge lives mostly in the thinking rooms, so adding rooms adds knowledge. But each word still visits only one or two, so answering stays fast. The receptionist, the router, is trained with an extra rule to share work fairly; otherwise it sends everything to one favourite expert and the others never learn.

My fun test was video-game lore: a closed world with its own words, so it's easy to see whether the specialists learned something the base model didn't know.
